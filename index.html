Drawing from the Crowd: A Citizen Science Platform for Mapping Ukiyo-e Cultural Geography

Welcome! Whether you are enthusiastic about art, love traveling Japan, or are curious about historical landscapes, we are glad you have found us. Through our project, we aim to bring together people from around the globe in order to jointly explore how ukiyo-e, Japanese prints (you might be familiar with the Great Wave or the Red Fuji as representants of this genre) depicted Japan’s famous places. And your participation helps us do that at scale! By joining our growing community of citizen scientists, you help reveal how historic prints of early modern Japan position viewers to experience Japan’s landscapes.

The platform launched in October 2025. It grew out of research at the University of Zurich (UZH)’s Chair of East Asian Art History, where The Nippon Foundation’s Social Innovation Collaboration project first supported its prototype (August 2024–February 2025), and the UZH Graduate Research Campus (GRC)’s Career Funding allowed its implementation (July 2025–June 2026).

By matching depicted landscapes with modern topography, we deepen our understanding of Japan’s historical, artistic and cultural geography while highlighting the importance of nuanced cultural preservation. Every contribution, yours included, helps scholars refine our understanding of spatial representation in early modern Japanese visual culture.

About the Project

How did the creators of historical images, print designers in the Edo period (1603–1868) and early photographers, position viewers to experience Japan’s famous landscapes? This question at the heart of Drawing from the Crowd is our invitation to curious minds from around the world: please help us to find the answer.

Why do we need to compare artistic with geographic data?

Print designers worked topography through convention, through what sold, through earlier pictures of the same place. What is interesting about ukiyo-e prints is that they did not conform to topographic ‘ground truth’, but instead combined information deriving from observed, from imagination, and from previous renderings of places in image and poetry. Some combine several views into one, or transport the viewer up to bird’s height to show a place’s spatial logic from a vantage no one could occupy. By contrasting the topography and its pictorial version, we notice the patterns of looking that the landscape asked, and asks, us, its viewers, to perform. We recover the historical gaze.

What does the project do?

Through georeferencing the prints with you, meaning through aligning prints against topography on a large scale and deriving insight from the contrast, we work to identify which elements of a print reflect actual topography and which stem from artistic convention. Two questions drive the work: how can we tell, collectively, which features are observed and which are conventional? And what does that reveal about how Edo-period prints positioned their viewers to experience Japan’s famous places (meisho)?

How the project began

“Drawing from the Crowd: A Citizen Science Platform for Mapping Ukiyo-e Cultural Geography” began as one of five projects selected for the 2024 Nippon Social Innovator’s Collaboration (NSIC), funded by The Nippon Foundation. The funder asked us to build something socially useful together with other scholarship alumni. That brief gave us the project, and it gave us something we did not expect: a team that thinks across fields. Building on this foundation, we secured implementation funding through a Career Grant from the Graduate Campus (GRC) at the University of Zurich (UZH), Switzerland.

The platform launched publicly in October 2025, and we began citizen science outreach in December 2025. We are now actively building our community of contributors while expanding our print collection through partnerships with institutions worldwide.

プロジェクト概要

歴史的な画像をつくった人々、すなわち江戸時代（1603〜1868年）の絵師や初期の写真家たちは、鑑賞者をどのような視点に置いて日本の名所を体験させたのでしょうか。「集合知を描く（Drawing from the Crowd）」の核心にあるこの問いは、世界中の好奇心あふれる方々へのお誘いです。ぜひ、その答えを一緒に探してください。

なぜ芸術のデータと地理のデータを比べる必要があるのか

絵師たちは、絵画的慣習を通じて、何が売れたかを通じて、そして同じ場所を描いた過去の絵を通じて、地形を描き上げていきました。浮世絵の興味深いところは、地形上の「グラウンド・トゥルース（正解）」に従うのではなく、観察されたもの、想像によるもの、そして絵や詩における過去の描写から得られた情報を組み合わせている点です。複数の眺めを一枚にまとめたものもあれば、鑑賞者を鳥の高さまで引き上げ、誰も立てない視点からその場所の空間的な論理を見せるものもあります。地形とその絵画的な表現を対比させることで、その風景が鑑賞者である私たちに求めた（そして今も求めている）まなざしの型が見えてきます。私たちは、歴史的なまなざしをたどり直すのです。

このプロジェクトは何をするのか

皆さんと一緒に版画をジオリファレンスすること、つまり版画を大きな規模で地形と重ね合わせ、その対比から洞察を引き出すことを通じて、版画のどの要素が実際の地形を反映し、どの要素が芸術的慣習に由来するのかを見分けていきます。二つの問いが作業を導いています。どの特徴が観察に基づき、どの特徴が慣習によるものかを、皆の力でどう見分けるか。そしてそれは、江戸時代の版画が鑑賞者をどのような視点で名所（meisho）へと導いたかについて、何を教えてくれるか。

June 2026 Dr. Stephanie Santschi Wrapping up the implementation phase means you can now find book illustrations, drawings and photos on the page in addition to prints! The collection doubles, and now includes sketchbooks, photographs and gazetteer pages, not just prints.

After collecting and processing more data (concretely, 1,345 additional records), we have now uploaded a second batch. This doubles the available collection which we can georeference together. Together with this upload, we have also formally wrapped up the GRC-funded Implementation Phase. Its result is this website, a bilingual platform on Smapshot, a small but growing community of contributors, and equally, a dataset that continues to expand and which has inspired first insight.

I am particularly excited that it now includes sketchbooks, photographs and gazetteer pages beyond the prints which we uploaded in a previous step. Remember, until June this was a collection of single-sheet ukiyo-e landscape prints. You will now find two Hiroshige sketchbooks from the British Museum, pages from meisho-zue (the illustrated gazetteers of famous places) from the National Diet Library and the National Archives of Japan, and a set of historical photographs.

These additional records widen the field for discovery and help us answer our research question, how the images mediate the gaze onto the landscape, through now allowing comparison between media. Think about it: even if referring to the same place, a camera, a hand drawing on the spot, and a publisher’s woodblock do something different to the same view. A photograph replicates the geometry it was made with: a camera stood somewhere, whatever the framing, and that precise location can be calculated. In comparison to that, a sketchbook page potentially follows paths and places to rest for a short while, if drawn in front of the thing. In comparison to that, the print studio, with its production team of the print designer, block-cutter, printer and the publisher, were following a sense of what would sell. And even further removed from the place, a meisho-zue illustration was ever understood as an informative illustration of a place, an image conveying spatial, historical, cultural or spiritual truths about a place, not topographical in a statistic sense, so all these media would have different things to say about the visual logic of a place.

Set them along that range, from geometry at one end to composition at the other, and the research question becomes tractable. We have been asking which parts of a print follow the terrain and which follow pictorial convention. Now we can put it across media, and where a sketchbook page and a finished print show the same view, we can watch the change happen and measure it. The photographs close a loop, too.

And while this part of the funding phase has ended, the project has not. Some funding applications that would take the project into the direction of tourism research and of collaboration with text-based citizen science are currently in preparation; and soon, I will also be able to announce a substantial, exciting step in my career.

And in the project, verification carries on, contributions are still very much wanted, and for that we of course invite you warmly! Start mapping →

December 2025 Dr. Stephanie Santschi From Prototype to Platform: Our Journey So Far The first post: how the project went from a six-month prototype to a working platform.

We are excited to launch the blog for Drawing from the Crowd! This space will share project updates, research insights, and stories from our growing community of citizen scientists.

Where It All Began

In August 2024, Drawing from the Crowd was selected as one of five projects for The Nippon Foundation’s Social Innovators Collaboration (NSIC). This six-month grant enabled our interdisciplinary team, spanning digital anthropology, spatial history, and Japanese art history, to develop a prototype platform for georeferencing ukiyo-e prints.

The challenge we set ourselves was ambitious: could we adapt existing crowdsourcing technology, designed for historical photographs, to work with stylized Japanese woodblock prints? Unlike photographs, ukiyo-e prints encode space through cultural conventions: artists might move Mt. Fuji for better composition, elongate beaches for dramatic effect, or combine multiple viewpoints into a single image.

Building the Prototype (August 2024–January 2025)

Working with our technical partners at Smapshot Lab (HEIG-VD), we adapted their monoplotting technology for Japanese prints. We sourced images from the Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Open Access collection and terrain data from Japan’s Geospatial Information Authority. The PI’s Postdoctoral Research at the University of Zurich’s Chair of East Asian Art History provided the art historical expertise to understand how Edo-period prints encoded spatial relationships and positioned viewers.

Beta testing revealed something exciting: while foreground elements in prints are often manipulated for artistic effect, middle and background terrain maintains consistent relationships with actual geography. This means citizen scientists can successfully georeference prints by focusing on terrain contours rather than iconic features like Mt. Fuji.
